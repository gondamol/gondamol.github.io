Research

I study what people with chronic conditions in Kenya pay for care, what insurance actually covers when they reach a clinic, and how facility finances decide whether medicines are on the shelf.

Most of my recent work uses the 2022 Kenya Demographic and Health Survey, the last national survey before NHIF was replaced by the Social Health Authority in October 2024, so these estimates serve as a pre-transition baseline. Alongside it I run mixed-methods facility studies and contribute data management and survival analysis to oncology research in western Kenya.

Themes

Health financing and Kenya's SHA transition

Cancer outcomes and HIV

Journal articles

  1. Financial determinants of effective hypertension and diabetes care in rural primary health facilities in Kisumu, Kenya: a mixed-methods study

    Amollo NW, Ogol J, Museve E, Owenga JA, Aduda DO, Onguru D

    BMC Public Health 26, 1275 (2026) First author

    Summary

    Seven public primary care facilities in Seme Sub-County all prepared workplans and budgets, but none had a dedicated NCD budget line or formal financial autonomy. Spending needed county approval that usually took three to four weeks, sometimes more than two months, so facilities could not buy locally when central supplies failed, and six of the seven reported frequent medication stockouts. Chronic care depends on facility decision space, not only on how much money there is.

Preprints

  1. Insurance coverage, healthcare use, and outpatient payment among adults with disability in Kenya before the SHA transition: a secondary analysis of KDHS 2022

    Amollo NW, Ogol J

    medRxiv, September 2026 First author

    Summary

    Of 40,197 adults in KDHS 2022, 6.7% met the Washington Group disability threshold. Adults with disability were more often uninsured, but that gap disappeared once wealth and education were accounted for. They used outpatient care and were hospitalised far more often under every adjustment, and 90.5% of outpatient users with disability paid something at their last visit, insured or not. The paper sets a pre-SHA benchmark and argues that SHA-era monitoring should measure payment at the point of care, not just enrolment.

  2. Type 2 diabetes remission in sub-Saharan Africa: why pragmatic lifestyle trials are a health system priority

    Amollo N, Ogol J, Owenga JA

    Qeios, September 2026 First author

    Summary

    Trials in China, India, the USA, the UK and Qatar show that structured lifestyle programmes can prevent diabetes and, in some adults with early disease, put it into remission. No remission-focused randomised trial has been identified in sub-Saharan Africa, where patients often present younger and many cannot reliably get the medicines they are prescribed. We argue for a phased agenda: feasibility work, pragmatic trials in primary care, and room in guidelines for remission-oriented research.

    Qeios

  3. Health insurance coverage, affordability barriers, and treatment gaps among Kenyan adults with diagnosed hypertension or diabetes before the Social Health Authority transition: a sex-stratified analysis of the 2022 Kenya Demographic and Health Survey

    Amollo NW, Hyera HF, Ouma OJ

    medRxiv, May 2026 First author

    Summary

    Among 1,932 adults already diagnosed with hypertension or diabetes, 47.7% had any insurance, mostly through NHIF, and 63.8% were not taking medication for at least one diagnosed condition. Insurance was concentrated among wealthier households, while treatment gaps exceeded 60% in every wealth quintile. Insurance was not strongly linked to smaller treatment gaps, which suggests enrolment alone will not close them without deeper benefits, outpatient medicine access and clinics ready for continuous NCD care.

    medRxiv
